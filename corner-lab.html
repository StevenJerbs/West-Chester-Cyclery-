What the friction circle shows. Two dots, one per tyre (F front, R rear), each scaled to its own tyre's grip: sideways for cornering (from the lean, shared by load), fore-aft for that tyre's braking or drive. Whichever dot crosses its ring is the end that lets go: front-heavy braking while leaned pushes F over first (a wash-out), a dragged rear brake pushes R (the rear steps out). A dot is a tyre's demand: sideways for cornering (from the lean), fore-aft for braking or drive. The ring is what the surface can give (μ·load). Every newton spent braking is a newton not available for cornering, so braking while leaned pushes the dot to the edge, the tyres slide, and the bike runs wide.
What fights the lean. A leaned front wheel's contact patch sits behind and to the side of the steering axis (trail), so front brake force twists the bars into the turn; through counter-steering that rolls the bike upright: the stand-up moment, proportional to front brake force × sin(lean). The spinning wheels add a gyroscopic righting moment (wheel angular momentum × yaw rate) that also lifts a turning bike, and gyroscopic damping that slows every roll change; braking spins the wheels down and so reduces that righting help while adding stand-up. The rider's counter-steer authority is finite, so when stand-up plus grip loss exceed it the lean collapses and the line opens.
Slow in, fast out. Brake in a straight line where the whole circle is available and the front tyre is loaded by weight transfer; bleed pressure off as lean comes on (trail braking) so the dot tracks the ring instead of crossing it; shift bias to the rear as the front's cornering load grows; be off the brakes at the apex so the lean can be at maximum where the corner is tightest; open the throttle as the bike stands up. Exit speed carries down the whole next straight.
