The model. The bike follows the surface; the rider's mass sits a distance h above it along the surface normal, on legs the rider drives. The only forces are gravity, the legs, and the ground pushing back along the normal. Energy is tracked exactly: the legs add energy at the rate force × extension speed, so the rider gains speed only when extending against a big load or compressing under a small one. In a trough the load is body weight plus the centripetal load m·v²·κ; over a crest it is body weight minus it. That is the whole trick: extend where the ground curves up (κ > 0), compress where it curves down. On the straight part of a slope κ = 0, so a push there only lifts your own weight and gives it straight back.
Angular momentum. In the trough the ground is an arc around a centre above you. Extending moves your mass toward that centre; with m·v·r conserved about it, a smaller r means a faster v. The readout shows m·v·r of the rider's mass about the current centre of curvature while the ground is curved.
The suspension is in the loop. The frame rides on the V10's two springs (21 kN/m together at the wheels, mid-clicks damping) between the ground and the rider's legs. The trough load compresses it, the damper turns part of every stroke into heat (the DAMPER LOSS readout), and only what the spring stored comes back. That is why scenario 4 locks it out: the same leg motion buys more speed on a rigid bike. Use the PLAYBACK slider to slow the transitions down and watch the arrows.
The transition taketh away. A passive rider (or a soft suspension) does the opposite: the centripetal load in the trough compresses the legs, work flows out, and the bike slows. Pumping is not free speed; it is the work the rider does against that load.
