PUMP LAB

Where the speed in a pump actually comes from, animated on Goldstone's V10: the solid rider does it right, the translucent ghost is the other technique on the same rollers, released at the same instant. Past the rollers the track drops into a tight set of doubles (3 m crest to crest) that pump speed lets the rider jump: the wheels leave when the ground curves away faster than gravity can follow, and what happens next comes down to the pop, the tuck and where the bike meets the dirt. After Seb Stott's How to Pump Your Bike, According to Physics (Pinkbike, 2021): it isn't pushing on the down-slope, it's moving your mass toward the centre of curvature where the ground curves. Same rider and bike as the Suspension Lab and Corner Lab.

The model. The bike follows the surface; the rider's mass sits a distance h above it along the surface normal, on legs the rider drives. The only forces are gravity, the legs, and the ground pushing back along the normal. Energy is tracked exactly: the legs add energy at the rate force × extension speed, so the rider gains speed only when extending against a big load or compressing under a small one. In a trough the load is body weight plus the centripetal load m·v²·κ; over a crest it is body weight minus it. That is the whole trick: extend where the ground curves up (κ > 0), compress where it curves down. On the straight part of a slope κ = 0, so a push there only lifts your own weight and gives it straight back.
Angular momentum. In the trough the ground is an arc around a centre above you. Extending moves your mass toward that centre; with m·v·r conserved about it, a smaller r means a faster v. The readout shows m·v·r of the rider's mass about the current centre of curvature while the ground is curved.
The suspension is in the loop. The frame rides on the V10's two springs (21 kN/m together at the wheels, mid-clicks damping) between the ground and the rider's legs. The trough load compresses it, the damper turns part of every stroke into heat (the DAMPER LOSS readout), and only what the spring stored comes back. That is why scenario 4 locks it out: the same leg motion buys more speed on a rigid bike. Use the PLAYBACK slider to slow the transitions down and watch the arrows.
The transition taketh away. A passive rider (or a soft suspension) does the opposite: the centripetal load in the trough compresses the legs, work flows out, and the bike slows. Pumping is not free speed; it is the work the rider does against that load.
The doubles. A half-roller drops the track into a set three times tighter than the rollers (3 m crest to crest, 55 cm deep, crests at the flat's height; the double black diamond on the far shoulder marks it). The bike stays on the ground only while the ground can push on it: over a crest the wheels need a pull of m·v²·κ toward the centre of curvature to follow it, and once that exceeds gravity's m·g·cosθ the whole stack leaves along the surface, bike and rider sharing the centre of mass's velocity (the suspension tops out first, so the frame and wheels go as one). From there the flight is ballistic. The legs can no longer add speed; they can only move the bike relative to the body (pulling the bike up 10 cm lifts it 8 cm and drops the body 2 cm), which is what a tuck is for. Two ways to leave the lip: let the crest throw you (scenario 5, rider A), or pump straight through it so the legs reach full length with the body still rising, which yanks the bike off the ground with the body's upward speed (rider B, and the pump rider in every other scenario). Landing is where the speed is kept or thrown away: the wheels keep only the velocity along the ground, so meeting the backside of the next roller costs almost nothing and its drop adds speed, while landing on its face (casing) kills the whole component into the dirt and folds the legs to their stop. Speed decides which you get: at the default entry the pop clears the gap; try 12 mph and watch the same technique land on the face of the next roller, 10 mph and it cases hard. Two honest caveats: the rigid-normal rider model is stretched on features this tight (the troughs are about as tight as the rider is tall), so the body's speed factor is capped between 0.8 and 1.05 of the wheels'; and a rider who runs out of speed pedals a stroke rather than stalling, booked in the LANDING + STOP LOSS readout.
