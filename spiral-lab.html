SPIRAL LAB

Where the speed in a pump comes from, done with the books open. A bike on a curved path, a rider on driven legs along the surface normal; the ground pushes only along that normal. On a circular arc the leg force points at the centre of curvature, so the angular momentum of rider and bike about that centre cannot change; yet the legs do work against the centripetal load, so the energy rises. Both are true at once, and the readouts prove it every step: the same thing in a trough, over a crest, round a berm, and on the zero-gravity magnetic pump track from the comments. After Seb Stott, How to Pump Your Bike, According to Physics (Pinkbike, 2021). Same rider and bike as the Pump Lab, Corner Lab and Suspension Lab.

On the arc the ground's push and the legs' push both point at the centre, so neither can twist the system about it. Gravity can (it has a lever arm about the centre everywhere but the bottom), so the readout subtracts gravity's integrated torque; on the zero-g track L holds exactly, whatever the legs do. With L fixed and I = m·r² shrinking as the rider moves in, L²/2I (the kinetic energy) has to grow. That is the ice skater.

Energy is not conserved: it is accounted for. The only thing that can add or remove it is the legs, at the rate force × extension speed. Push out against a heavy load and the total rises; let the load fold the legs and it falls. The mismatch readout is the integrator's honesty check.

A mass drawn toward the centre does not move on a circle any more, it moves on a spiral, and on a spiral the radial pull is no longer at right angles to the motion: it leans forward and does work. The angle readout is that lean. On a straight the curvature is zero, the load is only body weight, and a push just lifts the rider.

The model. Two masses from the Pump Lab: a 17 kg bike constrained to the path, and a 68 kg rider a distance h from it along the surface normal, on legs that push with a force F (the rider can also pull, as if clipped in, up to 1.2 body weights). Coordinates are the bike's distance along the path s and the leg length h; the equations come straight from the Lagrangian, so the ground reaction is a constraint that does no work, and the rider's velocity is ṡ(1 − κh) along the track plus ḣ along the normal, where κ is the signed curvature (positive when the centre is on the rider's side: a trough or a berm; negative over a crest). The generalised momentum ps = [mb + mr(1 − κh)²] ṡ is what the angular-momentum readout shows, multiplied by the radius: on a constant-radius arc with no tangential force it cannot change, whatever the legs do. The curvature ramps in and out over 60 cm at each end of the arc so nothing is discontinuous. Integration at 2 kHz; energy is checked, not assumed.
Trough, crest, berm. The trough is a U in the side view: a 15° down-slope, an arc of the chosen radius, a 15° up-slope. The crest is the same upside down, and there the centre is beneath the ground, so "moving toward the centre" means pulling the bike up into the body. The berm is the same arc seen from above with the bank carrying the weight: gravity plays no part in the turning plane, which is why pumping a corner is pumping a trough. Zero-G is the magnetic pump track from the comments: no gravity at all, and the technique still works, push-pumping troughs and pull-pumping crests.
Techniques. PUSH IN THE CURVE extends the legs only while the ground curves, finishing as the arc ends. PUSH ON THE SLOPE does the same extension on the straight before the transition and finishes a bike-length short of it, where the curvature is zero: the legs lift the rider against body weight and nothing else, the potential energy goes up by the same amount, and the rider arrives at the curve already tall with nowhere to go, so the speed gain at entry height is a fraction of the in-curve push's. PASSIVE leaves the legs as a soft spring: the load in the curve folds them, work flows out of the system and the bike leaves slower than it arrived (the transition taketh away). RIGID holds h fixed: no work either way, a baseline.
What the numbers say (10 mph in, 6 m radius, 30 cm stroke, Goldstone's 68 kg on the 17 kg V10). Trough on Earth: push in the curve, 281 J of leg work, +0.51 mph at entry height and posture (200 J of the work is banked as the taller posture's potential energy; 81 J became speed). Push on the slope: 208 J of work, +0.09 mph: it all went into height. Passive: −141 J and −0.87 mph, the transition taketh away. Rigid: 0. The same trough on the zero-g track: push in the curve +0.26 mph, push on the slope exactly 0, because with no gravity a push where the ground is straight lifts nothing and turns nothing. Berm: +0.42 mph from 75 J, no gravity involved, which is the article's point that a turn is a trough. Crest on Earth at this speed: the pull is worth +0.06 mph, because the load over a 6 m crest at 10 mph is still mostly body weight; on the zero-g track the same pull is worth +0.21 mph, kpickrell's pull-pump. Energy is accounted to within 10 J in every case.
What to watch. Pick the trough and PUSH IN THE CURVE, then read the three cards during the arc: L flat, L²/2I climbing exactly with the kinetic energy, ΔE − W at zero. Switch to PUSH ON THE SLOPE: the same work is done, the potential energy rises by the same amount, and the speed gain at entry height is gone. Then turn gravity off and do the crest with PUSH IN THE CURVE (which, for a crest, is a pull).
